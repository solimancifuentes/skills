# Calibration examples

All briefs and passages below are synthetic. They demonstrate choices, not factual claims about actual people or products. Each comparison uses the same source material and intended message. Adapt the behavior rather than recycling the wording. These examples are illustrations, not a compulsory template or independent validation.

## Concise explanation

**Brief:** A dashboard now exports a CSV containing only the rows selected by the active filters. Write one sentence announcing the change.

**Weak application:** The dashboard's new CSV export functionality provides the ability to export rows based on the filters currently applied.

**In voice:** You can now export your filtered dashboard results as a CSV.

**Why it works:** The reader gets the action and its scope immediately. A single sentence needs no setup, metric, heading, or explanation of an invented problem.

## Nontechnical explanation

**Brief:** A rain garden is a shallow planted area that collects runoff from a roof or driveway. Water soaks into the soil there, reducing the amount that reaches a storm drain. Explain it to a homeowner; no quantified performance claims are available.

**Weak application:** A rain garden is a shallow planted area for the collection of roof or driveway runoff, facilitating infiltration into the soil and a reduction in storm-drain inflows.

**In voice:** A rain garden gives water from your roof or driveway a place to collect and soak into the soil. It is a shallow planted area, so less of that runoff reaches the storm drain.

**Why it works:** Concrete objects and a visible sequence explain the function. Nothing needs to be described as a platform, workflow, or breakthrough for this voice to work outside technology.

## Forceful argument

**Brief:** Argue that a volunteer group should retire its approval form for routine supply purchases. The form repeats the same details already required in a receipt email and adds no information for the reviewer. Do not invent costs, delays, or motives. Offer no new policy.

**Weak application:** The group should consider retiring the routine-purchase approval form because it duplicates the information in the receipt email without providing additional information for the reviewer.

**In voice:** Retire the form. The receipt email already gives the reviewer the same information. Asking volunteers to copy it into another box does not make the purchase easier to judge. It just gives the paperwork a second home.

**Why it works:** A direct position leads into its reason, then a dry image lands the criticism. The sharper tone does not require inventing an incompetent reviewer, a hidden motive, or a numerical cost.

## Personal humor and reflection

**Brief:** The narrator brought a wooden stool to their first repair workshop. They spent twenty minutes choosing sandpaper. The instructor then pointed out that one leg was loose. The narrator felt embarrassed, put the sandpaper down, and worked on the leg. Write a short first-person passage with self-directed humor. Do not add dialogue, other participants, sensory details, or a later outcome.

**Weak application:** At my first repair workshop, I brought a wooden stool and spent twenty minutes choosing sandpaper. The instructor pointed out a loose leg. I felt embarrassed and stopped choosing sandpaper so I could work on the leg instead.

**In voice:** I spent the first twenty minutes of my first repair workshop choosing sandpaper for a wooden stool. The instructor pointed out that one leg was loose. I had given the finish a great deal of attention for someone who had yet to establish whether the stool could do its job.

I was embarrassed. I put the sandpaper down and worked on the leg.

**Why it works:** The humor comes from the mismatch already present in the facts. Sentence length changes as the narrator moves from explanation to embarrassment and action. The ending stays inside the supplied memory; it does not invent a successful repair or turn the moment into a universal rule for living.

## An exaggerated imitation

**Brief:** A repair café meets on the first Saturday of each month. Visitors may bring one broken household item. Volunteers assess whether a repair is possible. Write a short notice with those facts.

**Overdone application:** One Saturday. One item. One question: can it be repaired? The repair café meets on the first Saturday of every month. Bring one broken household item. Volunteers assess the possibility of repair. An item. An assessment. A possible repair.

**Correction:** The repair café meets on the first Saturday of each month. Bring one broken household item, and volunteers will assess whether it can be repaired.

**Why it works:** The correction preserves all the information and the uncertainty about repair. Short sentences, repetition, and rhetorical questions can provide emphasis, but piling them up gives a simple notice a theatrical weight it has not earned.

## Evaluation example

For the overdone notice, useful feedback would identify the repeated fragments, explain that they obscure a straightforward invitation, and offer the correction. It would not demand a numerical impact claim, a longer mechanism explanation, or a different opinion. Evaluate the fit of each choice to the actual assignment rather than counting stylistic devices.
