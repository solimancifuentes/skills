# Illustrative dimension anchors, version 2.0

These are **synthetic development examples**, not human-calibrated ratings, a validated test set, or universal numerical answer keys. They make the existing categories easier to discuss without changing the 57 patterns, their assignments, or the scoring formula. Apply [the scoring contract](scoring.md) to the actual task and complete assessed text; a short example below cannot establish the rating of a different document.

Each table varies a specified writing situation. Its rows illustrate plausible category assignments **if the described consequence and extent are supported**. Do not count matching words, snippets, or stylistic devices and look up a penalty. A zero requires review of the relevant scope; missing comparison material produces an unavailable status, not zero. An unavailable part of a dimension requires an explicit scope limitation.

## How to use these examples

1. Identify the task, audience, available materials, and actual reader consequence. Check the harmless explanation before recording a finding.
2. Record local impact and extent separately. Quote separated locations when claiming recurrence; state whether the locations exhaust the finding or merely illustrate it. A long quotation spanning a paragraph does not itself demonstrate several incidents.
3. Select the dimension category holistically. Explain which consequence and distribution make the adjacent category less suitable when the boundary matters. A finding's local impact need not equal the dimension rating.
4. If evidence is insufficient, preserve the uncertainty. Do not elevate a plausible concern to a supported finding to make the table fit. Do not lower a demonstrated serious consequence merely because the passage sounds natural.

The common categories remain: **0** no supported issue in reviewed scope; **1** minor isolated or occasional cost; **2** recurring cost or one substantial local defect; **3** widespread or multiple consequential defects; **4** dominance of the defect or a demonstrated major central breach. These are ordered descriptions, not measured equal distances. Do not multiply impact by recurrence or confidence. An optional rubric index is unvalidated and secondary; no example below introduces a score floor or a publication decision rule.

## Substance and specificity

**Situation:** a facilities manager asks why meeting-room reservations overlap and what the proposed change would do. The reply should explain the mechanism and consequences, not merely advertise a product. Relevant patterns: P01–P04, P33, P43, P46.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | “The two calendars accept reservations independently. A shared availability check rejects the second booking.” The reply connects the cause, proposed action, and relevant limitation. |
| 1 | One sentence calls this “an important step forward”; the surrounding explanation supplies the actual change. Removing that sentence would make a small improvement. |
| 2 | The reply says the change “improves scheduling efficiency” but leaves one important mechanism unexplained, or repeatedly restates that benefit in otherwise useful sections. The manager lacks a substantial part of the explanation. |
| 3 | Several sections enumerate features and benefits without explaining how they prevent overlapping bookings. The manager must reconstruct major parts of the argument. |
| 4 | Almost the entire reply consists of “unlock potential,” “transform operations,” and similar promises. It never provides the requested cause or mechanism; the central explanatory task remains unanswered. |

**Boundary and exception:** a product-feature inventory is appropriate if an inventory was requested. A short executive summary can point to an accessible mechanism explained elsewhere. For an omission finding, record the unmet request and the complete reviewed scope; do not invent a quotation for absent content. “Important” is not defective when the text establishes an important consequence.

## Grounding and epistemic discipline

**Situation:** supplied notes describe one team's trial: completion time decreased during the trial, there was no comparison group, and concurrent staffing changes could explain the result. Relevant patterns: P05, P15, P36, P53–P57.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | “Completion time decreased during this team's trial; the notes cannot separate the tool's effect from the staffing change.” The scope and uncertainty match the supplied record. |
| 1 | An incidental “teams generally welcome easier workflows” extends beyond the record but carries little argumentative weight. The trial's result and limitations remain accurately represented. |
| 2 | One supporting passage says “the tool caused the decrease,” while the main recommendation remains conditional and other sections correctly describe the uncertainty. The local causal claim exceeds the evidence. |
| 3 | Separate results and recommendation sections repeatedly treat correlation as causation and omit material alternatives. The account substantially overstates what the trial establishes. |
| 4 | The central recommendation says “the controlled trial proves the tool caused the improvement,” citing the supplied notes, and depends on that claimed proof. The record expressly lacks a control and identifies an alternative explanation. The central evidential basis is misrepresented. |

**Boundary and exception:** the same confident sentence cannot receive a source-based finding if the relevant source has not been read. It may instead need verification. A missing citation alone does not establish falsehood. Distinguish “unsupported by this record,” “contradicted by this record,” and an independently established false claim. Preserve a supported impact-4 grounding notice regardless of the optional aggregate. The most serious row is a central breach, not a rule that every causal overstatement receives 4.

## Diction and precision

**Situation:** a staff notice must tell nonspecialist colleagues how to request access to a shared folder. Relevant patterns: P19, P21–P26.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | “Ask the folder owner for access. Include your team name.” The words identify an action and actor appropriate to the audience. |
| 1 | One “at this point in time” replaces “now,” adding slight verbal padding without obscuring the action. |
| 2 | Several instructions use phrases such as “initiate an access enablement request” where a direct action is available, or one unexplained invented label obscures an important step. Readers must repeatedly translate the wording. |
| 3 | Across the main steps, the owner becomes “steward,” “custodian,” and “enablement stakeholder” without a real change of role. Jargon and shifting labels materially impede identifying who does what. |
| 4 | The notice is dominated by unexplained abstractions such as “activate cross-functional entitlement alignment.” The requested action cannot be recovered from the wording without substantial reconstruction. |

**Boundary and exception:** “entitlement” may be the exact interface label or a necessary technical concept for administrators. Preserve it when that precision helps the specified reader. A proper name, uncommon word, or repeated term is not a defect by itself. If the same wording also creates ambiguity, choose one primary dimension for that consequence; do not add a clarity penalty for the same repair.

## Rhetorical structure

**Situation:** a purchasing memo should explain a proposed contract change and its tradeoffs to a colleague. Relevant patterns: P06–P08, P10–P14, P16–P17, P20, P40.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | “The new contract lowers the annual fee but charges for support visits. It is cheaper if we need fewer than three visits.” The contrast expresses the actual tradeoff. |
| 1 | One “Let's be honest” delays a point that follows immediately and clearly. The added framing has little consequence. |
| 2 | The memo repeatedly stages simple disclosures with “The real question?” or “Here's what nobody tells you,” delaying otherwise substantive reasoning; alternatively, one forced opposition obscures a substantial tradeoff. |
| 3 | Multiple important objections are displaced by praise, false either/or framing, and unsupported claims to insider knowledge. Readers must disentangle the argument from the performance to evaluate the proposal. |
| 4 | The memo is dominated by flattery and an invented choice between “embracing the future” and “fearing progress.” It substitutes that framing for the requested evaluation of the contract throughout. |

**Boundary and exception:** a real either/or condition, a requested FAQ, or a useful teaching question can be appropriate. Courteous acknowledgment is not reflexive agreement when the response still evaluates the premise. A rhetorical frame and missing substantive analysis may coexist; record distinct consequences if making two findings, rather than charging twice for the same omitted argument.

## Rhythm and flow

**Situation:** an explanatory article should develop a connected account of a service failure. Relevant patterns: P18, P27–P28.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Sentence and paragraph structure follow the causal sequence. Parallel “First… Second…” steps make the sequence easier to follow. |
| 1 | One isolated “A delay. A setback.” adds an unnecessary dramatic beat, but the account remains easy to follow. |
| 2 | Several paragraph endings repeat short dramatic fragments, or several lists add a redundant third item solely to preserve a template. The interruptions create recurring, noticeable drag. |
| 3 | Nearly every section repeats the same opening, three-item list, and clipped ending despite different explanatory needs. The pattern repeatedly interrupts connections the article is trying to develop. |
| 4 | The account is dominated by disconnected beats such as “One queue. One pause. Everything changed.” Their repeated use prevents sustained development of the explanation across the passage. |

**Boundary and exception:** repeated openings can aid comparison, accessibility, or deliberate artistic effect. Three genuine items need no penalty. Sentence-length variation is not a target to maximize. Show how a pattern interrupts this reader's task; uniformity or a fragment count alone cannot justify a finding. If missing causal information is separately alleged, support that distinct substance consequence rather than relabeling the same rhythmic irritation.

## Clarity and information load

**Situation:** an administrator must copy a source folder to a backup folder and then remove one specified folder. The instructions name both folders. Relevant patterns: P29–P32, P34–P35.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | “Copy `source` to `backup`. Confirm the copy completed, then delete `source`.” Actors, objects, conditions, and order are recoverable without guesswork. |
| 1 | One aside interrupts a sentence, but its referents and the required order remain clear after a small reread. |
| 2 | One prerequisite is buried in a dense sentence, or a supporting instruction has two plausible referents that later context resolves. Readers must substantially reread that part. |
| 3 | Several steps leave essential actors, referents, or prerequisites unresolved. The administrator must reconstruct much of the procedure before acting. |
| 4 | The central instruction is “Copy the source folder to the backup folder, then delete it,” and no other text resolves whether “it” is the source or backup. The two readings produce materially different central actions. |

**Boundary and exception:** identify both plausible readings; do not label every pronoun ambiguous. A long sentence can clearly express one complex condition, and passive voice is appropriate when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. The final row supports a major central ambiguity, not a claim that data loss actually happened. One such demonstrated breach may outweigh numerous cosmetic difficulties in the narrative even without being a calculator-defined critical grounding/fidelity finding.

## Voice and editorial fidelity

**Situation:** the user requests light copyediting of an original customer update: “We expect to reopen on Friday, if the inspection passes. Until then, call Jo on 123.” Preserve meaning and the established conversational voice. Relevant patterns: P48–P52.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | The edit corrects punctuation while preserving the inspection condition, contact details, and suitable voice. The comparison materials permit review of the relevant checks. |
| 1 | One characteristic but harmless conversational phrase is replaced by a stiffer equivalent without a task-based reason. The message and most of its voice remain intact. |
| 2 | Several useful conversational choices are unnecessarily formalized, or a substantial local detail is omitted while the reopening condition and main message remain intact. |
| 3 | Multiple sections of a longer update lose distinctive phrasing or relevant supporting details despite the light-edit request. The resulting account requires several consequential restorations. |
| 4 | “We will reopen on Friday” replaces the conditional reopening statement. The edit removes the central inspection condition and creates a materially different commitment. |

**Boundary and exception:** a request to adapt the update for a formal audience may justify changes that a light-copyedit request would not. Newly added detail is unsupported by the original, not automatically proven false. With no original or relevant voice reference, do not infer flattening from a polished style. A voice sample alone does not establish what facts an edit added or removed. Show both sides of a meaning-change finding and preserve a supported impact-4 fidelity notice.

## Presentation and economy

**Situation:** the requested artifact is a standalone short announcement of an office closure and an alternative contact. Relevant patterns: P09, P37–P39, P41–P42, P44–P45, P47.

| Category | Illustrative evidence and consequence |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | A short heading identifies the closure date; the body supplies the date, affected service, and contact. Formatting helps the announcement stand alone. |
| 1 | “Here is your announcement” remains above an otherwise usable artifact, or one label repeats the next sentence. Removing it is a small cleanup. |
| 2 | Repeated headings, emphasis, and a nearby recap force unnecessary scanning in the short notice, or one substantial process digression delays the essential contact information. |
| 3 | Several layers of previews, workflow narration, teaching cues, and repeated summaries surround the announcement. Readers must search through much extraneous material to recover the usable artifact. |
| 4 | Almost the entire response is a drafting log, repeated framing, and decorative sections; the announcement's essential details are scattered inside it. The response requires extensive extraction to serve as the requested artifact. |

**Boundary and exception:** headings, boldface, emoji, and punctuation have no intrinsic penalty. A long report may need both a roadmap and a conclusion. A requested audit trail can properly foreground process. Do not exclude author-written framing from an artifact assessment merely to lower its burden; do exclude a quoted example when the declared task is to assess only the surrounding authorial prose.

## Difficult boundaries and disagreement

- **One consequential defect versus many small ones:** describe both rather than treating the larger count as automatically worse. One unresolved central instruction can justify 4; numerous harmless headings justify no finding. Repeated small costs can support 2 or, when their demonstrated accumulated effect is substantial and widespread, 3. Explain the effect rather than imposing a count threshold.
- **Local impact versus dimension extent:** one substantial local problem in an otherwise effective article can fit dimension 2. A demonstrated central breach can fit 4 even when its quotation is short. Conversely, a high local impact does not mechanically set every tagged dimension to the same category. Keep the primary dimension, impact, extent, and selected category inspectable.
- **Restricted evidence versus clean performance:** a grounding rating of 0 after checking visible overstatement does not establish factual accuracy of unverified assertions. State the restricted scope. An unsupported suspicion remains tentative and must remain visible as tentative, without becoming a scored finding or an unqualified clean verdict.
- **Minor difference between assessors:** if both 1 and 2 are plausible, record which reader cost or extent observation distinguishes them. Do not average away the disagreement before inspecting its cause. Independent study records retain each original judgment; adjudication is a separate record.
- **Changing context:** the same text can reasonably receive different judgments for different audiences or tasks. Document the changed condition. A response to a request for a list should not be rated as though it had been asked for a causal explanation.

In development, collect genuine target-text examples and harmless counterexamples alongside these synthetic aids. Obtain independent judgments before discussing them, inspect why adjacent categories differ, and revise ambiguous instructions on development material. Reserve separate unseen examples for later evaluation. Successful use of these tables, or agreement between model assessors, does not by itself establish scientific calibration.
